<div class="cheat-card">

#### Executive presence: pitch structure

Conclusion first (what you recommend) → why it matters to them (business
impact, one sentence) → the ask (what you need). Under two minutes, zero
jargon.

</div>

<div class="cheat-card">

#### Personal branding

Pick one specific, narrow area of expertise to be known for. "Engineering
leader" is too broad to be memorable; "fixes engineering-org scaling pain at
mid-size SaaS companies" is specific enough to be referred.

</div>

<div class="cheat-card">

#### Stakeholder alignment

Map stakeholders by what they actually need from you and in what register —
sales wants a confident simple answer, engineering wants the caveats, execs
want the business impact.

<span class="cheat-see">See: Adapting Communication Tone by Audience</span>

</div>

<Exercises>
<Exercises.Task title="Rewrite a rambling update as a three-part pitch" level="beginner" stretch="Time yourself reading it aloud and cut it until it takes under a minute.">

Rewrite this in the sheet's structure: **"We've been looking at the flaky test problem and there are a lot of factors, timing and environment and data, and we've tried a few things, and it's improving a bit, and it might be good to get some help at some point."** Assume the real recommendation is to dedicate two engineers for one month to fix the top causes, because flaky failures delay releases about a day a week.

**Done when:** the first sentence is the recommendation, one sentence states the business impact in plain terms, the ask says exactly what you need and for how long, there is no jargon, and the whole thing is under 120 words.

</Exercises.Task>
<Exercises.Task title="Say one message three ways" level="intermediate">

The release is delayed by two days because the payment tests are failing. Write the message for sales, for engineering, and for executives.

**Done when:** the sales version is confident and simple with no technical caveats, the engineering version includes the specific caveats and unknowns, the executive version leads with business impact and names the decision needed, and the three clearly differ in register rather than only in length.

</Exercises.Task>

<CaseStudy title="The ask on slide fourteen">
<CaseStudy.Context>

*Illustrative scenario.* An engineering lead presents to executives with a deck that builds carefully from background to analysis. The request for two more engineers appears on slide fourteen.

</CaseStudy.Context>
<CaseStudy.WhatHappened>

The executives spent the meeting trying to work out why they were being shown all of this. By the time the ask arrived, time was up and attention was gone, so the answer was to come back next quarter.

</CaseStudy.WhatHappened>
<CaseStudy.Lesson>

Lead with the conclusion, follow with why it matters to them in one business sentence, and then make the ask. Under two minutes and with no jargon, so the decision can be made in the room.

</CaseStudy.Lesson>
</CaseStudy>

<AISpark>

- Ask an assistant to compress a long update into recommendation, impact, and ask, then check it kept your real numbers and did not invent any.
- Have it rewrite one message for sales, engineering, and executives, and judge whether each version fits that audience's register.
- Ask it to critique your one-line description of what you are known for, and pick the version specific enough that someone could refer you for it.

</AISpark>
