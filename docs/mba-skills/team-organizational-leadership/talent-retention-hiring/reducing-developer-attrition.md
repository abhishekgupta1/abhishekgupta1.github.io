## Quick Reference

Regretted attrition is almost always driven by a small set of addressable causes: **unclear or blocked growth**, **a bad manager relationship**, **lack of autonomy/ownership**, **compensation falling behind market**, and **burnout**. Most of these show up in [1-on-1s](../situational-leadership-coaching/running-1-on-1s.md) months before someone quits — the fix is to notice and act on the early signal, not to react well at the exit interview.

## What is it?

Developer attrition is engineers leaving the team or company. Not all attrition is bad — some is healthy (a poor fit leaving, natural churn) — but "regretted attrition" (losing someone you wanted to keep) is a leading indicator of organizational health problems, and it's expensive: recruiting, onboarding, and lost institutional knowledge typically cost 6-9 months of the departing engineer's salary. Reducing it is mostly about treating the well-known causes as things to actively manage, not as unavoidable facts of the industry.

## When to Use

- Building a retention strategy proactively for a team or org
- After losing a valued engineer, to diagnose whether the cause is addressable or idiosyncratic
- Reviewing exit interview data for patterns across multiple departures
- As a lens on your own team: is there a quiet risk building with someone you'd hate to lose?

## Detailed Example

A senior engineer resigns. In the exit conversation, they cite "new opportunity," which is true but incomplete. Digging into the actual pattern over the prior year:

```
Signal (present for months before the resignation):
- In 1-on-1s, repeatedly mentioned wanting more ownership of a
  specific system; was told "maybe next quarter" three quarters
  in a row
- Hadn't had a level-up conversation in 18 months despite consistently
  strong performance reviews
- Their manager's 1-on-1s had become primarily status updates over
  the last few months (a sign the coaching relationship had thinned)

What a proactive team would have done differently:
- Treated "wants more ownership, told 'maybe next quarter' repeatedly"
  as a flag worth escalating, not a routine deferral — this is exactly
  the kind of blocked-growth signal that predicts regretted attrition
- Used the career progression framework to have an explicit
  conversation about what promotion would actually require, even if
  the timeline was genuinely not immediate — clarity, even
  disappointing clarity, retains people better than open-ended
  deferral
- Noticed the drift in 1-on-1 quality and course-corrected before it
  became one more reason to disengage
```

The resignation wasn't really about the "new opportunity" — it was about a year of small, unaddressed signals that ownership and growth were stalled here.

## Key Takeaways

- 💡 The five biggest addressable drivers: blocked growth, manager relationship quality, lack of autonomy, below-market compensation, and burnout — most exits are some combination of these, not one dramatic event
- 🔥 By the time someone resigns, you're usually 6-12 months too late to act on the actual cause — the leverage point is noticing the signal in 1-on-1s much earlier
- ⚠️ Counter-offers after a resignation rarely fix the underlying issue (they fix comp, not the other four drivers) — most people who accept a counter-offer leave within a year anyway
- ✅ Treat "I want more ownership/scope" mentioned more than once as a signal requiring a real answer, not a polite deferral — even a clear "not yet, and here's what needs to happen first" retains better than silence
- ⚡ Regular, honest calibration of compensation against market (not just internal equity) removes one of the five drivers proactively rather than reactively

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake**: Treating every departure as unpredictable or purely external ("they got a great offer").
**Why it fails**: It's comforting but usually wrong — most regretted attrition has visible precursors in 1-on-1 notes, engagement, and growth conversations, if anyone looks back at the pattern. Treating it as unpredictable means the same pattern repeats with the next engineer.

**Mistake**: Only addressing retention risk with a counter-offer once someone has a competing offer in hand.
**Why it fails**: It signals to the whole team that leverage, not internal advocacy, is what gets you a raise or promotion — which perversely encourages your best people to go interview elsewhere periodically just to reset their comp or scope.

## Advanced Usage

### Building a retention early-warning habit

Periodically (e.g., quarterly) review your team through a specific lens: for each engineer, "if they resigned tomorrow, would I be surprised, and do I know why they'd leave if not?" If you can't answer the second half, that's itself the signal to invest more in [1-on-1s](../situational-leadership-coaching/running-1-on-1s.md) with that person.

### Connecting retention to leveling and hiring quality

A legible [career progression framework](./building-career-progression-frameworks.md), consistently applied, directly reduces the "blocked growth" driver. A well-calibrated [interview loop](./crafting-tech-interview-loops.md) reduces attrition indirectly too — poor-fit hires create friction that drives out the strong performers around them.

## Scenarios & How to Respond

These cross all four audiences from [Adapting Communication Tone by Audience](../situational-leadership-coaching/adapting-communication-tone-by-audience.md) — the engineer (supportive), HR/skip-level (collaborative or concise depending on level), and upper management (data-driven, outcome-focused) when a pattern needs escalating.

**Scenario: A star engineer tells you they have a competing offer.**
Audience & tone: Direct report — supportive, curious, not transactional.
Response: Resist the reflexive counter-offer-only response. Ask directly: "Before we talk numbers, help me understand what's actually driving this — is it comp, scope, something about how we work together, or growth?" A comp-only fix to a scope or growth problem usually just delays the same resignation by a few months.

**Scenario: An exit interview reveals a systemic issue (e.g., multiple people cite the same manager or process).**
Audience & tone: Skip-level or HR business partner — concise, data-led, a clear ask for action.
Response: Don't treat it as one person's individual grievance if the pattern repeats. Escalate the aggregate pattern plainly: "Three exits in two quarters have independently cited the same issue — I'd like to see this addressed, not just acknowledged." A single exit interview is an anecdote; three with the same theme is a data point that should change something.

**Scenario: An engineer shows signs of quiet quitting — doing the minimum, disengaged in meetings, no longer proactive.**
Audience & tone: Direct report — supportive, non-judgmental, open-ended.
Response: Don't wait for a resignation to name it. Raise it directly in a 1-on-1: "I've noticed you seem less engaged lately than a few months ago — what's changed?" This is often the earliest visible signal of a core driver (blocked growth, burnout, manager fit) and is far cheaper to address now than after they've mentally checked out.

**Scenario: A compensation review reveals a strong performer is meaningfully below market.**
Audience & tone: Direct report (supportive, transparent) and upward to whoever approves comp (concise, evidence-based ask).
Response: Fix it proactively. Advocate for the correction through your normal comp process with a clear, data-backed case. If timing is constrained, tell the engineer directly: "I've identified that your comp has fallen behind market, and I'm actively working the correction" — rather than letting them discover it themselves via a recruiter.

**Scenario: An engineer wants to leave specifically because of friction with their manager.**
Audience & tone: Direct report — fully supportive; and separately, a candid conversation with the manager in question (direct, not accusatory).
Response: Take it seriously as a retention-critical signal. Ask the engineer: "Would an internal move to a different team be something you'd want to explore, if the friction is the core issue?" Separately and confidentially, address the pattern with the manager if it's likely to recur with others on that team.

**Scenario: Several departures cluster on one team in a short window.**
Audience & tone: The remaining team (supportive, direct listening) and upper management (concise, pattern-level framing with a recommendation).
Response: Don't evaluate each resignation independently — investigate the team as a system. Ask remaining team members directly: "What's felt different on this team lately?" When reporting up, frame it concisely: "Three departures this quarter on one team, common thread is X — recommending Y."
