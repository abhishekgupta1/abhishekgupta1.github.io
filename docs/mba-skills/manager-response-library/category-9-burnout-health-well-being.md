# Category 9: Burnout, Health & Well-being

**Part of**: Manager Response Library

---

## 41. A high-performing employee shows subtle signs of mental health struggles or personal crisis affecting their work

**Core Objective**: Support the person first, while still being honest about the impact on their work, without overstepping into a role you're not qualified for.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: You're not their therapist, and you don't need to diagnose anything — your job is to notice, ask with genuine care, connect them to real support, and adjust expectations temporarily where reasonable. Precision about what's happening is HR's or a professional's job, not yours.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Name the specific change you've observed, gently and privately.
2. Ask an open question and genuinely listen without pushing for details they don't want to share.
3. Point them toward real support (EAP, HR, professional resources) rather than trying to solve it yourself.
4. Adjust expectations or workload temporarily where reasonable, and follow up regularly but not intrusively.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I've noticed some things feel different lately — [specific, gentle observation] — and I wanted to check in, not to pry, just because I care and want to make sure you're okay."
>
> "You don't need to tell me any details you don't want to — but I do want you to know support is available, [EAP/HR resource], and I'm glad to help you access it if that's useful."
>
> "Is there anything about your workload right now that would help to adjust, even temporarily, while you're dealing with this?"

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- Prying for specific details about a personal or medical situation they haven't chosen to share.
- Trying to personally counsel or diagnose the issue instead of pointing to real professional support.
- Ignoring the signs because addressing it feels uncomfortable or overly personal.

## 42. The entire team is suffering from sustained crunch mode/sprint fatigue after a major launch

**Core Objective**: Actively recover team capacity and trust after a period of overwork, rather than immediately moving on to the next deadline.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: A launch's true cost includes the recovery time after it — skipping that recovery just borrows against the next sprint's output and the team's long-term trust that "crunch" is actually temporary.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Acknowledge the crunch explicitly and thank the team specifically, not with generic praise.
2. Build in real recovery time (reduced scope, time off, lighter sprint) immediately after the launch, not "eventually."
3. Debrief on what made the crunch necessary and what would prevent it recurring.
4. Protect the recovery period from being quietly eaten by the next urgent ask.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I know the last few weeks were genuinely hard — I saw the late nights and I don't want that to go unacknowledged. Thank you, specifically, for what each of you carried through this."
>
> "We're deliberately planning a lighter sprint next, on purpose — I want real recovery, not just a pause before the next crunch starts."
>
> "Let's talk honestly about what led to the crunch this time — was it scope, timeline, or something else — so we have a real shot at not repeating it."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "Great job, now on to the next thing!" immediately following a crunch period, with no acknowledgment or recovery time.
- Treating crunch as a normal, unremarkable part of the job rather than something to actively minimize.
- Letting the "lighter sprint" get quietly filled with new urgent work anyway.

## 43. An employee requests an extended medical, personal, or parental leave during a critical project milestone

**Core Objective**: Support the leave fully and without hesitation, while quietly and separately solving the project continuity problem — never make the employee feel responsible for both.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: The timing is your planning problem, not theirs to feel guilty about. Respond to the leave request itself with unconditional support, then handle the project risk as a completely separate conversation with the team.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Respond to the leave request with full, immediate support — no hesitation or subtle guilt.
2. Separately and privately, plan the project continuity — knowledge transfer, coverage, timeline adjustment.
3. Communicate the timeline or coverage change to stakeholders honestly, without naming the individual's personal reasons.
4. Keep the door open for how much (if any) contact they want during leave, respecting their preference either way.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Of course — I want you to take the time you need without any worry about work. Let's talk logistics only once you're ready; there's no rush."
>
> "I'll work out coverage and the project plan on my end — that's not something you need to solve before you go."
>
> "Whatever you'd prefer about staying in touch during your leave — fully off, or occasional light updates — is completely fine with me, just let me know what you'd like."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "This is really bad timing" or any variation that makes them feel guilty for the leave's timing.
- Asking them to fully document or hand off everything before leaving, adding stress right before departure.
- Expecting or pressuring availability during leave beyond what they've explicitly offered.

## 44. Setting healthy work-life boundaries for yourself as a manager so your team doesn't copy toxic overworking habits

**Core Objective**: Model the behavior you want the team to have — your visible habits set the real, felt norm far more than any stated policy.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: A team watches what their manager actually does, not what they say. Sending emails at midnight teaches "this is normal here" regardless of what you say about work-life balance in an all-hands.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Audit your own visible habits — after-hours messages, weekend activity, never taking real time off.
2. Make boundary-respecting behavior visible and explicit, not just private.
3. Use scheduling tools (delayed send, status indicators) so your own working hours don't implicitly pressure others.
4. Explicitly give the team permission and cover to set their own boundaries, backed by your own example.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I'm going to start scheduling any late-night or weekend messages to send during work hours instead — I don't want my working hours to accidentally set an unspoken expectation for the team's."
>
> "I took a real vacation last week and stayed fully offline — I want to be clear that's the standard I want for this team too, not just for me."
>
> "If you see me sending something outside normal hours, there's no expectation to respond until you're back online — I mean that genuinely."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "Do as I say, not as I do" — expecting boundaries from the team while visibly working around the clock yourself.
- Responding to your own messages sent off-hours in a way that implicitly signals urgency.
- Treating your own burnout as a badge of commitment rather than something to actively manage.

## 45. Reorganizing workloads when a key team member leaves unexpectedly during peak delivery

**Core Objective**: Redistribute the gap fairly and sustainably without silently overloading whoever's most willing to absorb it.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: An unexpected departure creates a real capacity gap — the instinct to "just redistribute and keep moving" without recalculating capacity is exactly how the remaining team quietly burns out covering for someone who's gone.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Recalculate actual team capacity honestly with the gap, rather than assuming the remaining team can simply absorb it.
2. Reprioritize scope explicitly against the new capacity — something has to give, and it should be a deliberate choice.
3. Distribute the redistributed work deliberately across the team, not by default to whoever's most willing.
4. Communicate the adjusted plan and timeline transparently to stakeholders.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "With [person]'s departure, our effective capacity just dropped meaningfully — I don't want to pretend we can absorb this without changing the plan."
>
> "Here's what I'm proposing for redistributing their work — I want to check this feels fair and sustainable to each of you, not just efficient on paper."
>
> "Given the capacity change, here's what I think needs to move on the roadmap — I want to be upfront about that rather than let it become an invisible overload on the team."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "We'll just have to pick up the slack" without any explicit recalculation or reprioritization.
- Defaulting the extra work to whoever's most willing or least likely to push back, rather than distributing it deliberately.
- Keeping the original timeline and scope unchanged despite the real capacity loss.
