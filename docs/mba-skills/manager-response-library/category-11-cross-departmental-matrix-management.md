## 51. Managing a project where you have accountability for delivery, but zero direct authority over cross-functional resources (Design, Legal, Security)

**Core Objective**: Deliver the project by influence and structured process instead of authority you don't have — make the dependency visible and negotiate it explicitly rather than hoping it resolves itself.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Authority you don't have can't be borrowed by insisting on it — it has to be replaced with something else: a clear shared stake in the outcome, an explicit ask with a deadline, and visible escalation when needed. Treat every cross-functional partner as a peer relationship to manage, not a resource to command.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Map every dependency explicitly — what you need, from whom, by when — and confirm it directly with each owner.
2. Frame every ask around their stake in the outcome, not just your deadline.
3. Build a shared visibility mechanism (a single tracker, a weekly sync) so misalignment surfaces early.
4. Escalate through your own management chain when a dependency is genuinely at risk — don't quietly absorb the risk yourself.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I don't own your team's time, so I want to make this ask as easy as possible to prioritize — here's specifically what I need, by when, and here's why it matters for [shared goal, e.g., the client renewal]."
>
> "Can we set up a quick weekly sync just on this dependency so we catch any slippage early, rather than finding out at the deadline?"
>
> "This dependency is now at risk of missing our shared date — I want to flag it to both our managers now, while there's still time to solve it together, rather than let it become a surprise later."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "I need this done by Friday" with no context on why it matters to them or their own priorities.
- Silently absorbing a slipping dependency and hoping it resolves, rather than escalating early.
- Going over someone's head immediately instead of trying a direct, collaborative ask first.

## 52. Resolving a stalemate between Engineering, Sales, and Marketing over feature priorities for an upcoming key client renewal

**Core Objective**: Break the stalemate with a shared, evidence-based prioritization process instead of whichever department argues loudest or has the most senior sponsor.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: A three-way prioritization fight usually means there's no shared framework or decision-maker, not that the underlying priorities are unresolvable. Introduce the framework and name the decider before trying to re-argue the specifics.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Get each side to state their case with actual data (revenue at risk, client commitments, feasibility) rather than assertions.
2. Apply a shared prioritization framework (e.g., RICE) so the comparison is structured, not political.
3. Name a single decider if the framework doesn't fully resolve it — usually whoever owns the renewal's revenue outcome.
4. Document the decision and rationale so it doesn't get relitigated by each department separately afterward.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Before we keep arguing positions, let's put actual numbers on the table — revenue at risk, technical effort, and confidence — so we're comparing apples to apples."
>
> "Here's how these three options score against that framework — if any of you think an input is wrong, let's fix the input, not skip the framework."
>
> "If we're still not aligned after that, [name] owns the renewal outcome and will make the final call by [date] — I want that decided now, not discovered later."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "Engineering says this isn't feasible, end of discussion." (asserting authority instead of showing the reasoning)
- Letting the loudest or most senior voice in the room win by default rather than the strongest case.
- Leaving the decision informally unresolved, letting each department act on their own assumption afterward.

## 53. Defending your team when another department publicly blames Engineering for a missed company revenue target or customer churn

**Core Objective**: Correct the record with facts, protect your team's morale and reputation, without turning it into a public interdepartmental fight.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Respond to the substance, not the tone — a calm, factual correction delivered directly to the people who need to hear it is far more credible and effective than a defensive public rebuttal.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Don't respond reactively in the same public forum — gather the actual facts first.
2. Address the inaccurate claim directly and privately with whoever made it, or their manager.
3. Present the facts plainly, including genuine contributing factors on your side if any exist.
4. Reassure your own team directly, so they don't absorb the blame silently.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I saw the claim that Engineering caused the churn — I want to walk through the actual timeline and data with you before this becomes the accepted narrative."
>
> "Here's what actually happened: [facts]. There were some things on our side we could've done differently too — [if genuinely true] — and here's what wasn't accurate about the broader claim."
>
> (To your team) "I saw what was said, and I want you to know I don't agree with that characterization, and I've addressed it directly. Here's what actually happened, for the record."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- Firing back publicly in the same channel, escalating an interdepartmental blame fight.
- Staying silent and letting the inaccurate narrative stand, leaving your team feeling unprotected.
- Overcorrecting into blaming the other department in return rather than sticking to facts.

## 54. Aligning with an overly aggressive Sales team promising custom features to clients without technical feasibility checks

**Core Objective**: Stop unchecked commitments from becoming your team's problem, by building a fast feasibility-check process Sales will actually use.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Sales isn't wrong to want to move fast for a deal — the fix isn't to slow them down generally, it's to give them a fast, reliable feasibility check so a "yes" from you is faster than a guess from them.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Address the pattern directly with Sales leadership, not just the individual rep.
2. Propose a specific, fast feasibility-check process (e.g., a same-day Slack channel or a 24-hour SLA) so it's a real, usable alternative.
3. Handle the specific already-promised commitment pragmatically — assess it honestly rather than punishing the client for the process gap.
4. Track the pattern — repeated unchecked promises after the process exists is a different, harder conversation.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I want to fix this so it doesn't put you or us in a bad spot with clients — can we set up a same-day feasibility check for anything you're about to promise, so you have a real answer before the client hears anything?"
>
> "On this specific commitment — here's what's actually feasible, on what timeline, and here's what isn't. Let's figure out together how to walk it back gracefully with the client if needed."
>
> "If this keeps happening after we've got the fast-check process in place, I'll need to raise it with your leadership directly, since it puts real delivery risk on both our teams."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "You can't promise things without checking with us first" as a blanket rule with no fast alternative offered — it just gets ignored under deal pressure.
- Publicly undermining the sales rep in front of the client instead of solving it collaboratively.
- Building the feature anyway to avoid conflict, reinforcing that unchecked promises get honored regardless.

## 55. Managing a co-leadership setup (e.g., dual engineering/product leads) where decisions are stalling due to unclear ownership

**Core Objective**: Restore decision velocity by clarifying decision rights explicitly, without dismantling the collaborative intent of the co-leadership structure.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Co-leadership fails most often not because two leaders disagree, but because neither has clear final say on any specific category of decision — ambiguity, not disagreement, is usually the actual bottleneck.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Diagnose whether stalls are from genuine disagreement or from neither party feeling empowered to decide.
2. Map decision categories explicitly (technical architecture, roadmap prioritization, hiring) to a named final decider for each.
3. Agree on an escalation path for the rare case both still disagree after that.
4. Revisit the split after a defined period to confirm it's actually working.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I want to understand — when a decision stalls, is it because you two genuinely disagree, or because neither of you is sure it's your call to make?"
>
> "Let's split decision rights explicitly: [product lead] has final say on prioritization, [eng lead] has final say on technical approach, and anything that touches both gets a joint call with a deadline."
>
> "Let's try this split for six weeks and check back in — if it's not working, we'll adjust rather than assume it's fixed forever."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "You two need to just work it out." (no structural fix, leaves the ambiguity in place)
- Assigning yourself as the tiebreaker for every disagreement, which undermines the co-leadership model rather than fixing it.
- Leaving decision rights implicit rather than writing them down and confirming both leads agree.
