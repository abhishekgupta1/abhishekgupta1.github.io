# Category 2: Conflict & Interpersonal Dynamics

**Part of**: Manager Response Library

---

## 7. Two senior team members disagree on architectural choices and hit a dead end

**Core Objective**: Get to a decision without either engineer feeling overridden by rank — preserve both the decision quality and the relationship.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Stop asking "who's right" and start asking "what would change your mind." If neither can answer, it's a values disagreement (risk tolerance, priorities), not a facts gap — no amount of further debate converges it. Your job is to name a decider and a deadline, not to personally settle the technical question.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Ask each engineer separately what would change their mind — surface whether this is fact-based or values-based.
2. Classify the decision's reversibility — a two-way door doesn't deserve more debate time.
3. Name a single decider (often whoever owns the long-term consequences) and a firm deadline.
4. Get explicit disagree-and-commit from the other party, out loud.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Let's stop trying to convince each other for a minute — what would actually change your mind here, for each of you?"
>
> "This sounds like a real values tradeoff, not a facts gap, so here's what I want to do: [name] will make the final call by Friday, after hearing both positions one more time."
>
> "Once the decision's made, I need both of you to build to it — if new information comes up later, bring it and we'll revisit, but not just because you still disagree."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "You're the more senior engineer, so we'll go with your call." (rank overriding merit)
- Letting the debate run indefinitely without ever naming a decider or deadline.
- Making the call yourself by default without involving the engineers closest to the tradeoffs.

## 8. A team member complains to you about another peer's work ethic or behavior

**Core Objective**: Get an accurate picture without becoming a relay for secondhand conflict — decide whether this needs direct action or coaching the complainant toward a direct conversation.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Most peer complaints are better resolved between the two people than through you as an intermediary — but you still need to know if it's a pattern worth your own intervention. Listen fully before deciding which path this needs.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Listen fully without agreeing or disagreeing in the moment.
2. Ask whether they've raised it directly with the peer yet.
3. If not, coach them on how to raise it directly first, unless it's serious enough to need your immediate involvement.
4. Track the complaint — a second, independent complaint about the same peer changes your calculus.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Thanks for telling me — help me understand what's actually happening, with specifics if you can."
>
> "Have you talked to them directly about this yet? A lot of the time that resolves faster than going through me, and I'm happy to help you think through how to raise it."
>
> "If this keeps happening after you've raised it, or if it's serious enough that you don't feel comfortable raising it yourself, come back to me and I'll step in directly."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- Immediately confronting the other peer with a secondhand account without verifying context.
- "That doesn't sound like them" (dismissing the complaint outright).
- Promising confidentiality you can't actually guarantee if the issue escalates.

## 9. Resolving friction between cross-functional teams (e.g., Engineering vs. Product Management)

**Core Objective**: Fix the structural cause of recurring friction, not just smooth over the latest flare-up.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Recurring cross-functional friction is almost always a structural problem (unclear ownership, misaligned incentives) wearing a personality-conflict costume. Fix the structure and the personal friction usually cools on its own.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Talk to each side separately first — understand what each team actually needs that they're not getting.
2. Name the structural gap explicitly, not as anyone's fault.
3. Negotiate a concrete, written agreement in a joint session.
4. Set a review date to confirm the agreement is holding.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Before we get into who did what, I want to understand — what do you actually need from Product that you're not getting right now?"
>
> "It sounds like the real gap is that there's no shared process for [specific gap] — let's fix that, not relitigate the last incident."
>
> "Let's write down exactly what each side commits to, and check back in six weeks to see if it's actually working."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "Product always does this to us." (adopting an us-vs-them frame instead of a joint-problem frame)
- Resolving it verbally in a meeting with no written follow-up.
- Taking either team's side, even privately — it makes you a target next time.

## 10. Managing a team member who openly challenges your authority or decisions in meetings

**Core Objective**: Address the behavior directly without shutting down legitimate dissent — the goal is respectful disagreement in the right forum, not silence.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Distinguish public challenge that's disruptive in style from genuine, valuable pushback. The fix usually isn't "stop disagreeing with me" — it's "let's find a better forum and tone for it." Shutting down all dissent teaches the team to stop raising real concerns.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Don't escalate in the meeting itself — acknowledge the point and move on if needed.
2. Follow up privately, promptly, and specifically about the pattern, not the content of any one disagreement.
3. Separate what's welcome (the substance of dissent) from what needs to change (the public, undermining delivery).
4. Offer an explicit, better channel for disagreement going forward.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I want to talk about what happened in the planning meeting. I'm genuinely glad you push back when you disagree — that's valuable. The way it came across today, though, felt like it undermined the decision in front of the team rather than debating it."
>
> "Can we agree that if you disagree with a call I make, you'll raise it with me directly first, or in the room in a way that doesn't read as a challenge to my authority itself?"
>
> "I don't want you to stop disagreeing — I want us to find a way to do it that's still productive for both of us."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "I'm the manager, that's my call." (asserting rank instead of addressing the actual behavior)
- Calling them out publicly in the same meeting, which escalates in front of the team.
- Punishing dissent broadly, which teaches the whole team to stop raising concerns.

## 11. Addressing microaggressions, exclusion, or inappropriate comments within the team

**Core Objective**: Act decisively and quickly to protect the affected person and set a clear behavioral standard, while following a fair process.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Silence is a decision too — not acting sends as strong a message as acting does, just the wrong one. Act promptly, take the report seriously by default, and separate "supporting the person affected" from "investigating and addressing the behavior," since both need to happen but not necessarily in the same conversation.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Thank the person for raising it and ask what support they need right now.
2. Investigate promptly and fairly, involving HR per your company's process for anything beyond a minor, first-instance issue.
3. Address the behavior directly and specifically with the person responsible.
4. Follow up with the affected person to confirm the issue is actually resolved, not just addressed once.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Thank you for telling me — I want you to know I'm taking this seriously. What would help you feel supported right now?"
>
> (To the person responsible) "I need to talk to you about something specific that happened in [context]. [Describe the specific comment/behavior and its impact]. This isn't okay, and it needs to stop."
>
> (Follow-up, to the affected person) "I wanted to check back in — has anything like this happened again, and is there anything else you need from me?"

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "I'm sure they didn't mean it that way." (minimizing impact by focusing on intent)
- Addressing it only vaguely to the whole team instead of directly with the person responsible.
- Failing to follow up after the initial conversation, leaving the affected person unsure if anything actually changed.
