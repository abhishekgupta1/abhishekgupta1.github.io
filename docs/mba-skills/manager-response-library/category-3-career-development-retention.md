## 12. A top performer demands an immediate promotion or raise that you cannot grant right now

**Core Objective**: Give a real, respectful answer that preserves trust — even though it's not the answer they want.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: Ambiguity is what actually damages trust here, not the no. A clear "not yet, and here's exactly what and when" retains people far better than a vague deferral. Treat the ask as legitimate even when the timing constraint is real.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Acknowledge their case and the strength of their work explicitly — don't undercut it while explaining the constraint.
2. Name the actual constraint honestly (budget cycle, level criteria, timing) without hiding behind vague language.
3. Give a specific path: what's needed, and by when you'd expect to make the case.
4. Set a concrete follow-up date so the conversation doesn't just evaporate.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Your work this year has been genuinely strong, and I want to be straight with you: I can't make this happen right now, and I want to explain exactly why, not just say 'maybe later.'"
>
> "The constraint is [budget cycle timing / a specific criteria gap] — here's exactly what I'd need to see, and here's when the next real opportunity to make this case is."
>
> "I don't want to leave this open-ended — let's put a specific date on the calendar to revisit, and I'll do my part to build the case between now and then."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "Let's see how things go." (vague, non-committal, erodes trust over time)
- Overpromising a timeline you don't actually control to avoid the hard conversation now.
- Making the case sound purely political ("it's just not the right time") without a substantive, specific reason.

## 13. A strong team member gives notice that they are resigning for a higher-paying offer

**Core Objective**: Respond with genuine support rather than a reflexive counter-offer, while understanding what's really driving the decision.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: A counter-offer that only addresses comp usually just delays the same resignation by a few months if the real driver was scope, growth, or management fit. Understand the full picture before reacting.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Congratulate them genuinely — resist any defensive or guilt-inducing reaction.
2. Ask what's actually driving the decision, beyond just the number.
3. If there's a genuine, fixable gap and you can act on it, offer to — but only if it addresses the real cause, not just comp.
4. Regardless of outcome, plan the transition and knowledge handoff constructively.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Congratulations — I know that wasn't an easy decision, and I appreciate you telling me directly."
>
> "Before we talk logistics, I'd genuinely like to understand what's driving this — is it purely the comp, or is there something about the role, the growth path, or how we work together that's part of it too?"
>
> "If there's something real here I can actually fix, I want to know — but only if it's the right decision for you either way, not just to keep you a few more months."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "I can't believe you're doing this to the team right now." (guilt-tripping)
- Jumping straight to a counter-offer before understanding the actual driver.
- Becoming visibly cold or disengaged for their remaining time, which damages the relationship and the team's trust in how departures are handled.

## 14. An employee feels stuck, unmotivated, or unsure about their career growth path

**Core Objective**: Turn a vague feeling of stagnation into a concrete, actionable growth plan.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: "Stuck" is usually a symptom of unclear next steps, not a lack of desire to grow. Your job is to make the path concrete — vague reassurance ("you're doing great, don't worry") makes the stuck feeling worse, not better.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Ask them to articulate what "growth" would actually look like to them specifically.
2. Map that against the team's actual opportunities and the career framework's criteria.
3. Identify one concrete next step or project, not just a conversation.
4. Set a check-in cadence to track progress, not just a one-time conversation.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "When you say you feel stuck, help me understand what that looks like for you — is it about scope, skills, recognition, or something else?"
>
> "Here's how that maps to what the next level actually requires — let's find a specific project in the next quarter that gives you a real shot at demonstrating it."
>
> "Let's not leave this as a one-time conversation — I want to check in on this specifically every few weeks, not just at the next review."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- "You're doing great, just keep doing what you're doing." (reassurance without a concrete path)
- Letting the conversation end without a specific next action.
- Assuming you know what "growth" means to them without asking.

## 15. Conducting a productive 1-on-1 career progression and goal-setting alignment meeting

**Core Objective**: Leave the meeting with a shared, specific understanding of where they're headed and what needs to be true to get there.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: This meeting works best as a two-way negotiation of shared understanding, not a one-way download of your assessment. Ask before you tell.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Ask them to share their own view of their trajectory and goals first.
2. Share your own honest assessment, anchored in the career framework's specific criteria.
3. Jointly identify 1-2 concrete goals for the next period, with a way to measure progress.
4. Document it somewhere both of you can refer back to.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "Before I share my view, I want to hear yours — where do you see yourself headed, and what does the next year look like to you?"
>
> "Here's how I see it, anchored against what the next level actually requires — [specific strengths, specific gaps]."
>
> "Let's agree on one or two specific things to focus on this period, and how we'll both know if it's working."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- Turning the meeting into a monologue of your assessment without asking for theirs first.
- Vague goals like "keep growing" or "take on more" without specifics.
- Skipping documentation, so the plan is forgotten by the next 1-on-1.

**Related Records**: [Running 1-on-1s](../team-organizational-leadership/situational-leadership-coaching/running-1-on-1s.md), [Building Career Progression Frameworks](../team-organizational-leadership/talent-retention-hiring/building-career-progression-frameworks.md)

## 16. Delegating high-visibility projects to mid-level engineers to build their leadership capacity

**Core Objective**: Give real ownership and visibility, not just extra work, while managing the risk of an unproven owner on a high-stakes project.

**Mental Model / Leadership Principle**: The point of the delegation is their growth, not just your capacity relief — that changes how much support and visibility you build in around it, compared to just assigning the task.

**Step-by-Step Action Strategy**:
1. Frame the opportunity explicitly as a growth stretch, not just a task assignment.
2. Set the delegation mode appropriately — likely Coach mode for a first high-visibility project, not full Empower mode.
3. Actively create visibility for their work with stakeholders, rather than absorbing the credit yourself.
4. Debrief afterward specifically on what they learned and how it maps to their next step.

**Exact Word-for-Word Script (Do's)**:
> "I want to give you ownership of this — it's a chance to work directly with [stakeholder] and show what you can do at a bigger scope. I'll be close by for the first design review, then stepping back."
>
> "When we talk to leadership about this project, you're presenting it, not me — I want you to get the visibility."
>
> "Let's debrief after this wraps — what worked, what was harder than expected, and how this connects to what you want next."

**Phrases to Avoid (Don'ts)**:
- Assigning the project but presenting the results to stakeholders yourself, capturing the visibility instead of passing it on.
- Full Empower mode with no check-ins on a genuinely high-stakes first attempt, setting them up to fail.
- Framing it purely as "I need help" rather than "this is for your growth" — the framing changes how it's experienced.
