## Quick Reference

Keep 3-6 months of expenses in a cash reserve — consulting income is lumpy and client payment terms (often net-30 or net-60) mean revenue lags work by months. Set up a formal entity (e.g., an LLC or Pvt Ltd, depending on jurisdiction) to separate personal and business liability before taking on your first paid client, not after. Every engagement needs a written contract covering scope, payment terms, IP ownership, and liability — a handshake agreement is the single most common source of consulting disputes.

## What is it?

This is the financial and legal infrastructure underneath an independent consulting practice — distinct from the client-facing skills of pricing and delivery, this is what keeps the practice itself solvent, protected, and compliant. It covers managing genuinely lumpy cash flow, choosing and setting up the right legal entity, structuring contracts that protect both sides, and understanding enough tax basics to not get an unpleasant surprise at filing time.

## When to Use

- Before taking on your first paid consulting client
- Setting up recurring financial habits (invoicing, reserve management, tax set-asides) once the practice has steady income
- Negotiating a new contract, especially one with unusual terms (IP assignment, non-compete, liability caps)
- Reassessing entity structure as income grows past initial thresholds where a different structure becomes more tax-efficient

## Detailed Example

Setting up the financial and legal foundation for a new solo consulting practice:

```
Entity setup:
  Chosen structure: single-member LLC (or local equivalent, e.g.,
  a Private Limited company) — separates personal assets from
  business liability, which matters the moment you're giving advice
  a client could claim caused them financial harm.
  Timing: set up before the first paid engagement, not after —
  liability protection doesn't apply retroactively to work done
  before the entity existed.

Cash flow management:
  Client payment terms: net-30 typical, sometimes net-60
  Reality: if invoiced at project completion, cash may not arrive
  for 4-8 weeks after the work (and the associated expenses) happened
  Reserve target: 3-6 months of personal + business expenses held
  in reserve, sized larger than a typical salaried employee's
  emergency fund specifically because of this lag and because
  consulting income is inherently lumpier (feast/famine between
  engagements)

Contract essentials (every engagement, no exceptions):
  - Scope: specific deliverables and explicit exclusions (what's
    NOT included, to prevent scope creep)
  - Payment terms: amount, schedule (e.g., 50% upfront, 50% on
    delivery), and what happens if payment is late
  - IP ownership: who owns the work product — commonly the client
    owns deliverables specific to their business, while your
    general frameworks/methodology remain yours
  - Liability cap: limiting your liability to (commonly) the fees
    paid, protecting against outsized claims relative to engagement size
  - Termination clause: how either party can end the engagement
    early, and what's owed if they do

Tax basics:
  Set aside a percentage of every payment (varies by jurisdiction
  and structure, often 25-35% as a rough planning estimate) into a
  separate account for tax obligations — consulting income
  typically isn't withheld automatically the way salaried income is,
  and treating 100% of an invoice as spendable income is a common,
  costly mistake for new independent consultants.
```

## Key Takeaways

- 💡 Set up a formal legal entity before your first paid engagement — the liability protection it provides doesn't apply retroactively, so delaying setup leaves early work personally exposed
- 🔥 Size your cash reserve for consulting-specific lumpiness (payment lags, gaps between engagements), not a generic personal-finance rule of thumb — 3-6 months is a reasonable starting point, more if client concentration is high (one client is a large share of income)
- ⚠️ Never start paid work without a signed contract, even for a trusted referral or a "quick" engagement — scope and payment disputes are far more common (and far more damaging) than most new consultants expect, and a contract is cheap insurance against them
- ✅ Set aside tax funds from every payment as it arrives, not at year-end — treating the full invoice amount as spendable income is one of the most common financial mistakes new independent consultants make
- ⚡ Revisit entity structure and tax approach as income grows — thresholds exist in many jurisdictions where a different structure becomes meaningfully more tax-efficient, and it's worth a periodic check with an accountant rather than assuming the initial setup remains optimal indefinitely

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake**: Starting client work on a verbal agreement or a brief email exchange instead of a signed contract.
**Why it fails**: Without a written scope, payment terms, and IP clause, a disagreement about what was promised or who owns the output has no clear resolution — this is the single most common source of consulting disputes and is entirely preventable with a standard contract template.

**Mistake**: Spending invoice income as if it were fully take-home, without setting aside for taxes.
**Why it fails**: Unlike salaried income with automatic withholding, consulting income usually requires the consultant to proactively set aside and remit taxes — spending the full amount as received routinely leads to a painful shortfall at filing time.

## Advanced Usage

### Structuring retainers for cash-flow smoothing

Beyond their pricing benefits (see [Value-Based Pricing & Client Acquisition](./value-based-pricing-client-acquisition.md)), retainers with upfront monthly billing directly address the cash-flow lag problem — a mix of project work and retainer relationships smooths income more reliably than project work alone.

### Managing client concentration risk

If one client represents more than ~30-40% of revenue, treat that as a financial risk to actively manage (diversifying the client base, negotiating longer notice periods in the contract) — client concentration is one of the most common reasons an otherwise healthy consulting practice suddenly destabilizes.

## Scenarios & How to Respond

**Scenario: A client wants to start work immediately and pushes back on the delay of getting a contract signed first.**
Audience & tone: Client/stakeholder — pragmatic, reassuring, firm on the non-negotiable, per [Adapting Communication Tone by Audience](../../team-organizational-leadership/situational-leadership-coaching/adapting-communication-tone-by-audience.md).
Response: "I completely understand the urgency — I can turn the contract around within a day, and it protects both of us equally if anything about scope or timeline shifts. Let's get it signed today so we can start with everything clear."

**Scenario: A mentee/newer consultant asks whether they really need an LLC for a small, part-time practice.**
Audience & tone: Direct report / mentee — supportive, developmental, honest about the tradeoff rather than a blanket rule.
Response: "It depends on your risk exposure and local setup cost — walk me through what kind of advice or work you're doing, since that affects how much liability protection actually matters here." Help them reason through the specific tradeoff rather than giving a one-size-fits-all answer.

**Scenario: A client disputes an invoice, claiming the delivered work didn't match what was agreed.**
Audience & tone: Client/stakeholder — calm, evidence-based, non-adversarial where possible.
Response: Go back to the signed contract's scope section first: "Let's look at what we agreed to in the scope section together — I want to understand specifically where you feel it diverged." A clear contract usually resolves this quickly; where it doesn't fully cover the disputed point, that's useful information for tightening the next contract template.
