## Quick Reference

Price against the **value the client gets**, not the hours you spend — a two-week engagement that saves a client $500K is worth far more than $150/hour × 80 hours, and pricing it that way leaves money on the table while also anchoring the client's expectations on your time instead of their outcome. Structure proposals around the client's problem and outcome, not your process. Negotiate retainers around a defined scope and outcome, with explicit boundaries on what's in and out.

## What is it?

Value-based pricing charges for the outcome delivered rather than the time worked, in contrast to hourly billing, which caps your income at your available hours and inadvertently rewards inefficiency (the slower you work, the more you bill). It requires being able to quantify or credibly estimate the value of the outcome to the client — which connects directly to the diagnostic and analytical skills covered elsewhere in this domain — and requires client acquisition and proposal-writing skills that sell the outcome, not the process.

## When to Use

- Pricing a new engagement, especially one with a quantifiable business outcome (cost savings, revenue impact, risk reduction)
- Writing a proposal for a prospective client
- Negotiating an ongoing retainer relationship
- Deciding whether to take on a project priced hourly vs. one priced against value, when a client offers a choice

## Detailed Example

Pricing an engagement to diagnose and fix the onboarding bottleneck from the [Consulting Frameworks](./consulting-frameworks.md) example:

```
Hourly framing (weak):
  "3 weeks of consulting at $200/hour, roughly 120 hours = $24,000"
  Problem: anchors the client on your time, invites hour-by-hour
  scrutiny, and caps your fee regardless of how much value the fix
  actually delivers.

Value-based framing (stronger):
  Step 1 — Quantify the value:
    Current onboarding takes 6 weeks avg; each week of delay
    correlates with a measurable increase in early churn. Estimated
    value of cutting onboarding time by 50%: ~$400K/year in reduced
    early churn, based on the client's own historical data.

  Step 2 — Price as a fraction of that value, not as hours:
    Propose $35,000 for the diagnostic + implementation roadmap
    phase — roughly 9% of the estimated first-year value, a
    defensible ratio common in outcome-based consulting, while still
    being a smaller number than the value delivered.

  Step 3 — Structure the proposal around their problem, not your process:
    "Your onboarding bottleneck is costing an estimated $400K/year in
    early churn. Here's a 3-week engagement to diagnose the specific
    cause and deliver a prioritized fix roadmap." (Their problem and
    the outcome, stated first — the methodology is in an appendix,
    not the headline.)

  Step 4 — Retainer negotiation for ongoing work:
    If they want ongoing support implementing the roadmap, propose a
    monthly retainer scoped explicitly: "$8,000/month for up to 2
    implementation check-ins and unlimited async advisory — scope
    beyond that (e.g., hands-on execution work) billed separately."
    Explicit boundaries prevent retainer scope creep, which is the
    most common source of retainer relationships going sour.
```

## Key Takeaways

- 💡 Quantify the client's value before naming a price — a number grounded in their own data (churn cost, revenue impact) is both a stronger pricing anchor and a stronger proposal argument than a generic day-rate
- 🔥 Lead every proposal with the client's problem and the outcome you'll deliver, not your process or credentials — clients buy outcomes; process detail belongs in an appendix, not the opening page
- ⚠️ Don't let a retainer's scope stay implicit — "ongoing advisory support" without defined boundaries reliably expands until it feels like more work than the fee justifies, on both sides' account of what's fair
- ✅ Price a range, not a single number, when the outcome is genuinely uncertain (e.g., "$30-40K depending on scope discovered in week one") — this is more honest than false precision and still anchors well above hourly framing
- ⚡ Track your own realized value-to-fee ratio across engagements over time — if you're consistently pricing well below 5-10% of quantified client value, that's a signal you're under-pricing relative to what the market and your own track record support

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake**: Defaulting to hourly billing because it feels safer or easier to justify.
**Why it fails**: Hourly billing caps upside regardless of outcome quality, invites clients to negotiate down your rate rather than your value, and structurally rewards slower work — none of which serves either party well over time.

**Mistake**: Writing a proposal that leads with your background, methodology, and process before stating the client's problem and the outcome.
**Why it fails**: A client evaluating a proposal is asking "will this solve my problem," not "how impressive is this consultant" — leading with credentials instead of their specific problem reads as generic and makes the proposal easy to skim past.

## Advanced Usage

### Handling a client who insists on hourly billing

Offer a hybrid: a fixed price for a clearly-scoped diagnostic phase, with hourly billing only for open-ended implementation work that's genuinely hard to scope in advance — this captures value-based pricing where the outcome is quantifiable while still accommodating a client's preference for hourly on the harder-to-scope portion.

### Connecting pricing confidence to your diagnostic frameworks

The [consulting frameworks](./consulting-frameworks.md) used to diagnose a client's problem are also what let you credibly quantify the value of fixing it — a vague diagnosis produces a vague, hard-to-defend price; a rigorous one produces a number the client can verify against their own data, which is what actually makes value-based pricing work rather than feel like a guess.

## Scenarios & How to Respond

**Scenario: A prospective client pushes back that your value-based price seems high compared to a competitor's hourly quote.**
Audience & tone: Client/stakeholder — pragmatic, reassuring, reframing rather than discounting, per [Adapting Communication Tone by Audience](../../team-organizational-leadership/situational-leadership-coaching/adapting-communication-tone-by-audience.md).
Response: "I understand the number looks different from an hourly quote — this price is scoped against the ~$400K in value we estimated this fixes, not against hours worked. Happy to walk through how we got to that estimate so you can evaluate it against the outcome, not just the fee."

**Scenario: An existing retainer client keeps expanding the scope of "ongoing advisory" without additional payment.**
Audience & tone: Client/stakeholder — direct but professional, not accusatory.
Response: "I want to keep delivering well on what we've scoped — I'm noticing recent asks have gone beyond the retainer's original boundary. Let's revisit the scope together so it's clear what's covered and what would need a separate arrangement." Name it plainly rather than letting resentment build silently.

**Scenario: A less experienced consultant you're mentoring is underpricing every engagement out of fear of losing the client.**
Audience & tone: Direct report / mentee — supportive, developmental, per the coaching register used across this knowledge base.
Response: "What do you think this client would lose if the problem went unsolved — have you actually quantified that yet?" Walk them toward pricing confidence through the value-quantification exercise itself, rather than just telling them to "charge more."
